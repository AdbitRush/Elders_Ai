A plan is something you can change

Evidence checked 3 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

Some puzzles are enjoyable because you can look ahead. If you move this piece now, where could the next one go? The first plan does not have to be perfect. Trying a route, noticing a problem and changing direction can be part of the game.

Planning is more than waiting before a move

A study of 154 community-dwelling older adults used a task involving preparation for a hypothetical day out. Participants completed it with or without an initial planning condition. The researchers found no significant overall differences between those conditions in accuracy, efficiency or time. [1]

Within the study, however, the quality of the initial plans was informative. More efficient plans predicted more efficient execution, and plans connecting relevant aspects of the tasks predicted shorter execution time after adjustment for some other factors. These relationships do not prove that telling everyone to plan first will improve everyday functioning. [1]

The result is a useful caution: adding a "think first" screen is not the same as teaching a useful strategy, and a planning puzzle is not proof of a real-world benefit.

Explore the choices without a penalty

A comfortable puzzle can show the goal, let you preview a sequence and offer Undo. You might ask which move keeps another option open, or whether two errands could share a stop. If the plan does not work, try a different order.

A fictional town is a good setting for this kind of play. Visit the library before it closes, collect a picnic basket and finish at the park. The game can explain which rule a route missed. Nothing needs to happen against a countdown.

These are puzzle ideas, not an assessment of your ability to manage appointments, medicines or money. Real-life decisions can involve information and consequences a game does not capture.

The bottom line: Planning can make a satisfying puzzle. Treat its routes as something to explore and revise, not a clinical score or a promise of better daily functioning.
