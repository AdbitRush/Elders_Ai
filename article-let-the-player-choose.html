Let the player choose

Evidence checked 3 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

Some days a small puzzle is enough. On another day, you might want more pieces or a longer sequence. A visible choice can make the difference between playing at your own pace and feeling pushed by the software.

A small study of control

A 2015 study compared two versions of a tablet memory game in 21 healthy older adults over three weeks. One version gave more control over difficulty, rewards and visual themes. Participants in that version played more often and for longer, and performed better within the game. [1]

The limits matter. This was a small study with pseudo-random allocation and no inactive control group. Three design elements changed together, so the study cannot say which element caused the difference. More practice may also help explain better game performance. The four transfer tests did not show a difference between versions. [1]

It is not evidence that an untimed toggle improves memory, or that every game needs the same levels. It is a useful reason to take player choice seriously and test the actual experience.

Choices should be clear and reversible

A game can show its difficulty before a round begins and explain what changes: more objects, a longer pattern or another rule. A lower level should not be described as less healthy. If a change needs a fresh round, say so before resetting anything and let the player cancel.

An untimed option is a comfort and accessibility choice, not a treatment. A pause button, reminder, mute control and solution reveal should be easy to find. Keep those choices available without an account or a demand to share health information.

Good usability still needs observation. Can someone understand the first move, recover from a mistake and finish with help? A setting is not useful just because it exists in a menu.

The bottom line: Player control deserves care. A small game-design study supports further testing, not a claim that customizable BrainPlay games improve general thinking or health.
