Give a new name something to connect to

Evidence checked 3 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

A new name can seem to disappear while you are still enjoying the conversation. One possible approach is to give it a connection: a word, a detail or a simple image that helps you distinguish it from the other names you have just heard.

A connection is a cue. It is not a guarantee, and it does not have to be clever.

What a mnemonic study can tell us

A 1984 study taught a face-name mnemonic to younger, middle-aged and older adults. The published abstract reports that all three groups improved their name recall after learning it, even though overall recall differed across age groups. We reviewed that abstract, not the full paper. It does not establish long-term everyday effects or show that every association technique works equally well. [1]

Other face-name research also shows why support matters. In experiments comparing repeated testing and study, older adults benefited from testing when they received feedback during learning. [2] An association is not a reason to keep rehearsing a wrong name without checking it.

Keep the connection kind and simple

For a fictional practice character named Rose, a picture of a rose could provide a straightforward cue. For a real person, a detail they choose to share might be more natural: "Sam likes sailing." You can repeat the name in conversation when it fits, or ask again if you need to.

These are suggestions, not the precise method tested in the 1984 study. Avoid judging someone's appearance or inventing personal details to make a cue. The point is a useful reminder, not a label that would embarrass them.

A practice game should use fictional characters rather than ask you to upload real people's faces or names. It can show a cue, let you hide it and offer a reminder whenever you want. You should not have to share a private memory to play.

The bottom line: Name associations can be an interesting strategy to try. The evidence here supports a narrow learning task, not a promise to improve every conversation or diagnose memory problems.
