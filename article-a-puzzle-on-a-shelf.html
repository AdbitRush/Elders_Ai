A puzzle on a shelf: the railroad sidings game

Facts checked 4 November 2026 by opening the sources listed below. English only.

Please note: this is a short history note, for interest. BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

A switching puzzle asks you to rearrange freight cars with a locomotive on a small set of tracks, where each track holds only a few cars. Model railroaders build them because even a layout the size of a shelf can hold a real puzzle.

Two well-known puzzles

Two well-known layouts are Timesaver, created by John Allen and first published in the magazine Model Railroader in November 1972,3 and Inglenook Sidings, created by Alan Wright.1

In the classic Inglenook, three sidings hold five, three and three cars, and the short track the locomotive uses has room for the locomotive and three cars. Eight cars sit in the sidings, and the task is to make up a train of five chosen at random, in a set order. The track plan is based on real sidings at Kilham, on a branch line of the North Eastern Railway.1

The puzzle has also been studied mathematically: a 2018 paper by Simon Blackburn works out when such a rearrangement can always be done, and looks at how to do it in the fewest moves.2

Our version

BrainPlay's Rail Yard Shuffle is an original game in the same family. Its hard level uses the classic five-three-three sidings with eight cars and a train of five. To keep it simple to play, one move sends the engine into a siding and out again with the number of cars you choose, rather than steering each push and pull. Every puzzle can be solved, and there is no clock.

In short: a handful of short tracks makes a surprisingly deep puzzle, which is why model railroaders keep building them.
