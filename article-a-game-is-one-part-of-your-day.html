A game is one part of your day

Evidence checked 2 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

You might enjoy a familiar card game after breakfast, a puzzle with a friend or a new challenge when you feel curious. A useful play routine is one that fits your life. It does not need a streak, a perfect score or a promise to keep your brain young.

Keep the bigger picture in view

The National Institute on Aging's cognitive-health guidance covers much more than mental activity. It includes taking care of physical health, being physically active, getting enough sleep, managing blood pressure and other health conditions, and addressing hearing or vision problems. Staying connected with other people matters too. [1]

A game should not crowd out those parts of life. Research on a particular cognitive-training programme is not a reason to replace a walk, a conversation or needed medical care with extra screen time.

Make play comfortable

Try a game because it interests you. Choose a pace you like and pause when you have had enough. If a timer makes the game unpleasant, choose a different challenge. If you enjoy learning new rules, try something unfamiliar; if you prefer a familiar favourite, that is a valid choice too.

These are suggestions for enjoyment, not clinically tested instructions. The studies cited here do not establish how many minutes of BrainPlay someone should play, or how often, for a health benefit.

A score is not a diagnosis

An improving game score can show that you are getting used to that game. It does not establish a general improvement in memory. Equally, a difficult round is not a diagnosis of a cognitive problem. The National Institute on Aging warns against assuming that commercial brain-training applications have the same effects as training tested in clinical trials. [1]

If you are concerned about changes in memory or thinking, discuss them with a health professional rather than using a game score to judge your health. Do not change medication based on game performance. NIA advises discussing medicine concerns with a clinician rather than stopping prescribed medicines on your own. [1]

The bottom line: Enjoy the game for what it offers today. BrainPlay is entertainment and an opportunity for practice, not a medical assessment, treatment or proven dementia-prevention programme.

Source

  1. National Institute on Aging. Cognitive Health and Older Adults. Reviewed June 2024. https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/brain-health/cognitive-health-and-older-adults
