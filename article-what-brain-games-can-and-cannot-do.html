What can brain games really do?

Evidence checked 2 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

A card game, a puzzle or a pattern challenge can be worth playing because you enjoy it. You do not need a medical promise to make time for a pleasant game. But if you are wondering whether games improve memory or protect against dementia, the research needs a closer look.

Getting better at a task is not the same as improving everything

Researchers often study cognitive training: structured practice designed to work on a particular thinking skill. That is different from simply playing any game that feels challenging.

In the large ACTIVE trial, older adults received training in memory, reasoning or processing speed. At the ten-year follow-up, reasoning and speed training still showed benefits on the abilities that had been trained. The memory-training benefit was no longer maintained at that point. Participants in all three training groups reported less difficulty with everyday activities, but the study did not find a training benefit on performance-based tests of everyday function. Only 44% of the original sample remained in the ten-year assessment. [1]

A 2020 Cochrane review of eight trials found small possible short-term benefits from computerised cognitive training, but rated the evidence low or very low quality. It did not establish a lasting overall benefit a year after training. Its scope was training lasting at least 12 weeks, and its search ended in 2018. It is not a review of every later study or every type of game. [2]

A promising new result, with important boundaries

A 2026 ACTIVE follow-up linked trial participants to 20 years of Medicare records. A specific subgroup receiving adaptive visual processing-speed training plus later booster sessions had a lower hazard of a recorded dementia diagnosis. The overall comparisons for each training arm were not statistically significant, and memory or reasoning training did not show the same benefit. [3]

This is an important research lead, not proof that ordinary games prevent dementia. The outcome came from insurance claims rather than individual clinical adjudication, and the authors describe selection and other limitations. BrainPlay was not the intervention tested in these studies.

A reasonable way to play

Choose a game you like, learn its rules and enjoy becoming more familiar with it. Treat an improving score as progress at that game, not a medical test. There is no validated BrainPlay schedule or daily dose for preventing cognitive decline.

The bottom line: Games can offer enjoyment and practice. Specific research programmes have produced promising results, but those results do not establish that BrainPlay improves general memory, treats cognitive problems or prevents dementia. [1-4]
