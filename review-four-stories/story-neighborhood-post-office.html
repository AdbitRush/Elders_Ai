Main Street - 1960s

The little post office that kept us close

Lick-and-stick stamps, brass box doors, and a birthday card that traveled five hundred miles.

A trip worth taking

Before email and tracking numbers, a trip to the neighborhood post office could connect a family to nearly anyone in the country, or across the world. There were birthday cards to stamp, bills to mail, money orders to buy and holiday packages to send before the deadline. An errand that sounds ordinary now was, in its day, how love and news got from one town to another.

The post office also worked the other way. It was where the outside world arrived. In small towns especially, it doubled as a meeting place: essential service delivered with familiar faces, and a dependable chance to see a neighbor and trade the local news while you were at it.

At the service window

The line at the service window moved at its own pace, and nobody much minded. Behind the counter, a clerk who knew half the town by name weighed your envelope on a little scale, because an extra ounce changed the price. You bought a sheet of stamps, licked and stuck them, and pressed each one down with a thumb. The taste of stamp gum is one of those memories that never entirely leaves a person.

There was a service for everything. Certified Mail gave you proof that something important was sent and received. Registered Mail added extra protection for the truly valuable. Special Delivery promised prompt delivery by a messenger at the far end. And C.O.D., collect on delivery, let the recipient pay when the parcel arrived. For a few coins' worth of postage, distance became something manageable.

The wall of little doors

In the lobby stood the post-office boxes, row upon row of small brass-fronted doors, each with its own number and tiny window. Customers rented a box, turned a key or worked a little combination, and peered in to see what the day had brought. Reaching into that cool metal space was a daily small ceremony.

The lobby had its self-service side, too. A stamp-vending machine stood ready when the window line was long or the counter was closed. And the traveler without a fixed address could still be reached, thanks to General Delivery: mail addressed to a person, care of the post office, and held there until they came through town to claim it.

Red, white and blue around the edges

Some mail announced itself before it was even opened. An airmail envelope came edged in red and blue, on paper so light it felt like it might fly on its own. Airmail once carried a separate rate and a separate glamour: your letter would travel by air, which still meant something.

Then there was the postcard, vacation news in a few handwritten lines, message and address sharing one exposed card. It cost less to send, it showed a picture of somewhere wonderful, and it almost always ended with the same faithful sentence: "Wish you were here."

The flag, the slot and the blue box

Not everything required a trip downtown. On rural routes, the mailbox by the road had a small flag with a big job: raise it, and the carrier knew there was outgoing mail waiting inside. In town, many front doors had a mail slot, and the day's letters dropped straight into the house, sometimes onto a waiting dog.

And on the corner stood the blue collection box, solid as a fireplug, its pickup times printed on the front. You pulled the handle, dropped your letter through the slot, and the door closed with a reassuring clang. That sound meant the letter was on its way. It was out of your hands now, and somehow that felt good.

Every envelope told a story

Look closely at old mail and you can read its journey. The postmark recorded where and when a letter entered the system, a small official stamp of place and date. Dark cancellation lines crossed the stamp itself so it could never be used twice. A careful return address in the upper-left corner was insurance: if a letter could not be delivered, it could at least come home.

The addresses themselves lived in a little book near the telephone or the writing desk. That address book recorded a family's whole geography: moves, marriages, new babies, new ZIP Codes after 1963. Entries were updated, crossed out and rewritten over the years, and the book grew more valuable with every change.

December at the post office

The holidays tested the whole system, and everyone knew the deadlines. Packages were wrapped with particular care: sturdy cartons, strong string, labels written clearly, because December sorting rooms tested every knot. Inside might be a sweater knitted over months, cookies packed in popcorn, or the one gift a grandchild had asked for by name.

The line at the window in December was long, cheerful and resigned all at once. People weighed their parcels, bought their stamps and wished the clerks a Merry Christmas, because the clerks were neighbors too. Then came the best part: the days of watching the mailbox for the cards coming back.

What mail were you happiest to find?

Try remembering what a good mail day felt like. A letter from someone far away, in handwriting you knew before you saw the name. A birthday card with a dollar tucked inside. A package with your name on it. What piece of mail were you always happiest to find waiting?

Ask someone you wrote to, or who wrote to you, what they remember. Somewhere in a drawer or a shoebox, those letters may still be waiting to be read one more time.
