Main Street - 1950s

A stool waiting at the soda fountain

Chrome stools, a cherry Coke after school, and the hum of the mixer behind the counter.

More than a drugstore

The neighborhood drugstore once offered much more than prescriptions and toothpaste. Near the front window, or tucked along one wall, a gleaming counter ran the length of the room: swivel stools, syrup pumps in a row, and the steady hum of the milkshake mixer. You could run in for aspirin and come out having split a sandwich with a neighbor.

The man or woman behind the counter had a title all their own: the soda jerk. The name came from the quick pull, the little jerk, that worked the soda-water tap. They knew the regulars, remembered the usual orders, and could build a sundae while carrying on three conversations.

A language of its own

Ordering at the fountain had a vocabulary you learned by listening. A cherry Coke was an ordinary cola transformed by a pump of cherry syrup, long before flavored colas filled store shelves. A phosphate had a clean, tart bite. A lime rickey was sharp and fizzy. And an egg cream, at least in New York, famously contained milk, chocolate syrup and seltzer, but no egg at all. Nobody could quite agree on why it was called that, which somehow made it better.

Then there were the ice cream drinks. A milkshake was thick and frosty. Ask for a malt and you got malted milk powder stirred in, a toasted, nutty flavor its fans considered essential. An ice cream float arrived with a scoop bobbing in fizz, creamy on top and bubbly underneath, demanding both a straw and a spoon.

The second helping in the metal cup

A proper milkshake came with ceremony. The soda jerk raised a stainless-steel cup around the spindle of the tall mixer, and the machine did its loud, wonderful work. Then your glass arrived full, with the metal cup standing beside it, holding the extra that did not fit. That little bonus pour felt like a gift every single time.

Perhaps you remember the tall parfait glass that needed an extra-long spoon, so no streak of syrup at the bottom went to waste. Or the sundae crowned with whipped cream, chopped nuts and one bright maraschino cherry. Or the banana split in its long boat-shaped dish, three scoops lined up between the halves, impressive enough to share, although plenty of people preferred not to.

The counter after school

For teenagers, the fountain was headquarters. After school the booths filled up, coins were counted out on the Formica, and one malt with two straws quietly announced a date. A tabletop jukebox selector sat in the booth, its little pages of song titles flipping under your thumb. A nickel, a button pressed, and your song joined the line.

The appeal was simple arithmetic: an affordable treat and room to visit. You could stretch a small amount of spending money across a whole afternoon of talk. For children, the chrome stools were entertainment enough. They spun. Turning in circles on the stool was nearly as good as the ice cream, right up until a grown-up asked you to stop.

Lunch at the counter

The fountain fed people, too. Grilled cheese with tomato soup, a hamburger, a plate of eggs: simple food served fast, with a little service bell ringing when an order was up. Shoppers at the five-and-dime or the department store could turn an errand into a treat just by sitting down at the counter for twenty minutes.

The room itself was part of the pleasure: bright laminate counters, chrome trim, vinyl seats, everything easy to wipe clean and shining under the lights. It looked modern then. Now it looks like a whole era, kept in perfect condition in our memories.

A first job behind the counter

For many of us, the fountain was also a first job. You learned to work the taps, to scoop ice cream that had hardened like concrete, and to keep your patience when the after-school rush hit all at once. The apron, the paper hat and the smell of syrup stayed with you long after the paycheck was spent.

Perhaps you still remember your best customer, or the trick of dipping the scoop in warm water. A skill like that never fully leaves a person. Hand someone a scoop today, and the old motion comes right back.

What was your order?

Try bringing back your counter. Was it in a drugstore, a five-and-dime, a department store? What did you order when you had coins of your own to spend: a cherry Coke, a phosphate, a float? And who sat beside you on the next stool?

Ask an old friend what they remember ordering. You may find that two people can share one counter, and still have two completely different favorite sundaes.
