Home & everyday life - 1960s

When the telephone stayed at home

One phone, one place in the house, and a ring that the whole family heard.

One phone, one place

There was a time when "the telephone" meant one familiar object in one familiar place. It rested on a hallway table, hung on the kitchen wall, or held a position of honor in the living room. Some families gave it furniture of its own: a little telephone table or gossip bench, with a seat, a shelf for the directory and a pad for messages.

The phone did not follow you around. You went to it. When it rang, everybody heard it, and somebody called out, "I'll get it," already walking. If no one was home, the phone simply rang. There was no missed-call list, no record, no way to know. The caller just tried again later.

The sound of dialing

Making a call had its own small ritual. You put a finger in the hole beside each number and turned the dial to the stop, then let it click patiently back before the next digit. A number full of nines and zeros took real commitment. Lifting the receiver brought the dial tone, that steady hum that meant the line was ready, and a busy signal meant exactly one thing: hang up and try again.

Perhaps you remember that some numbers did not start with digits at all. They began with exchange names, Murray Hill or Klondike, spoken as words before they were dialed as letters. The phone book, thick White Pages and even thicker Yellow Pages, sat by the phone doing the work a search bar does now.

The party line

In many communities, especially in smaller towns and out in the country, the line itself was shared. A party line meant several households on one circuit, each with its own ring pattern: two short rings for the Millers, a long and a short for you. Courtesy was part of the service. So, everyone knew, was the temptation to listen.

You learned to keep calls brief when a neighbor might be waiting for the line. And you learned, the hard way, that a quiet little click in the middle of your conversation meant somebody else had picked up.

Long distance was an occasion

A long-distance call was not made lightly. Charges climbed with time and miles, so the call was planned, the clock was watched, and the whole family sometimes gathered to take turns saying hello. Many households waited for the lower evening and weekend rates, and an unexpected long-distance ring could make a room go quiet with worry before anyone answered.

There was etiquette to it, too. Station-to-station was the cheaper choice: you talked to whoever picked up. Person-to-person cost more but began charging only when your named person came to the phone, which made it useful when a family shared one line. And the collect call had its own quiet genius. An operator announced the caller's name and asked whether you accepted the charges. Some families arranged whole coded messages this way: the call you declined was itself the news that everyone had arrived safely.

The people who connected the calls

Behind many of those connections was an operator. Dialing 0 reached a real person who could place a call, untangle a wrong number or find a listing. In the exchanges, rows of operators worked the switchboards, cords in hand, connecting one voice to another all day long.

Away from home, the telephone booth was the outpost of the same system. You closed the folding door against the street noise, dropped in your coins, and hoped for a dial tone. Everyone knew to check the coin-return slot. Every so often, somebody's forgotten dime was waiting there like a tiny gift.

Privacy was a longer cord

When a second phone came into the house, it was an extension on the same line, and it changed everything. Now a call could be taken in a bedroom or a den, though someone else could always lift another receiver, quietly, and sometimes not so quietly. "I've got it, you can hang up now" was a complete family sentence.

For teenagers, privacy was measured in feet of cord. An extra-long handset cord could stretch from the kitchen wall to a closet, a stairwell, or just around the corner, which felt like real independence. The cord paid a price. It twisted and kinked until the only cure was to let the receiver dangle and spin itself straight, a repair that never lasted.

Every ring was a surprise

Before caller ID, the ring announced that someone was calling and nothing more. The only way to know who it was, was to answer. That gave the telephone a kind of suspense we have traded away: every ring was a small mystery, and the voice on the other end was the reveal.

The answering machine changed that, eventually, and recording the family greeting could be a self-conscious little performance, done over until everyone stopped giggling. But for years, the arrangement was simpler. The phone stayed home. If you were out, the world waited.

Who were you always glad to hear?

Try remembering the sound of your family's phone: where it hung or sat, how its ring differed from the neighbors' on the party line. Who answered fastest? Who stretched the cord down the hall? And whose voice on the line could turn an ordinary evening into a good one?

Call someone whose number you still know by heart. There is a fair chance they remember yours, too.
