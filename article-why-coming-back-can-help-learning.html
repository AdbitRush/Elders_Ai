Why coming back can be different from starting over

Evidence checked 3 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

Repeating an answer immediately can feel easy. Returning to it after something else has happened can feel quite different. Researchers call practice separated by intervals "spaced" practice. Practice crowded together is often called "massed" practice.

The gap is part of the task

A 2023 paper examined learning unfamiliar face-name pairs in younger and cognitively healthy older adults. Its abstract reports benefits of spacing at longer retention intervals, with similar patterns of forgetting across age groups among spaced-trained participants. That finding concerns the names and faces used in the experiments, not an improvement in memory for everything. Our review checked the university's publication record and abstract. [1]

The details of a gap matter. A separate experimental paper compared different retrieval schedules in younger and older adults. Older adults had more difficulty when early attempts were separated by too much intervening material. An early, short gap could matter before moving to longer intervals. The experiments did not identify one ideal schedule for every person or every type of learning. [2]

A gentle way to revisit something

If you are learning a game's symbols, you could look at one, put it aside briefly, and see whether you remember it after another turn. Check the answer rather than leaving an uncertain guess unresolved. If the gap makes the task frustrating, shorten it or look again.

These are optional ways to explore learning. They are not a recommended treatment schedule. There is no evidence here for a required number of daily BrainPlay sessions, a perfect interval or a streak that protects your health.

A game can make revisiting convenient by keeping the same small practice set available. It should also let you stop. A missed day is not a failed health goal, and a forgotten answer is not a diagnosis.

The bottom line: Returning to material after a gap can change how it is learned. The research supports studying spacing carefully, not prescribing a universal browser-game routine.
