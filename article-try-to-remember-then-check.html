Try to remember, then check

Evidence checked 3 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

There are two different ways to use a practice card. You can look at the answer again, or hide it and try to bring it to mind. The second approach is called retrieval practice. It is a way to study information, not simply a way to measure what you know.

Feedback changes the picture

In two experiments, researchers compared repeated study with repeated testing of unfamiliar face-name pairs in middle-aged and older adults. In the first experiment, people did not receive feedback during the learning phase. Middle-aged adults benefited from repeated testing, but older adults did better with repeated study. In the second experiment, when feedback was provided, both groups benefited from repeated testing. [1]

That is a more useful finding than "testing is always best." The task, the learner and the chance to see the correct answer all matter. These were controlled experiments on particular face-name pairs, not a trial showing that a quiz improves everyday independence.

An answer should help you learn

A friendly practice game can show the correct answer after your attempt and give you time to look at it. It can explain a rule instead of displaying only a red cross. It can also offer "Show me" before you answer, without treating that choice as a failure.

Try a small example. Look at a fictional character and their name. Hide the name, try to recall it, then reveal it. Notice whether you were certain, partly certain or guessing. You do not need to type quickly or compete with someone else's score.

Seeing a correct answer is different from pretending you knew it all along. Both can be useful information for deciding what to revisit. A score can describe that round; it cannot tell you the state of your brain.

The bottom line: Trying to retrieve information can be useful practice, but the answer and feedback deserve as much care as the question. BrainPlay's implementation would still need its own testing before anyone could claim a learning benefit.
