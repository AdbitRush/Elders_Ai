The moon landing you typed in

Facts checked 4 November 2026 by opening the sources listed below. English only.

Please note: this is a short history note, for interest. BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

Most people who remember a lunar landing game picture an arcade cabinet. The idea is older than that, and it began as text on a computer terminal.

A student program from 1969

Jim Storer wrote the first version in the fall of 1969, as a student at Lexington High School in Massachusetts. It ran on a Digital Equipment Corporation PDP-8 minicomputer and was written in a language called FOCAL.1,2 Accounts say he was 17 and had watched the Apollo 11 landing that summer.2

There was no picture of the lander. The computer reported the situation in numbers, and the player decided how much fuel to burn for the next stretch of the descent, typing one answer at a time; one history of early text games describes the answer as the fuel to burn "in the next ten-second interval".3

How it spread

The program went out through Digital Equipment Corporation's channels for users, and David Ahl later translated it into the BASIC language, which helped it travel to many more machines.1,2 A decade later, in 1979, Atari released the arcade game Lunar Lander, with vector graphics on screen.1

Our version

BrainPlay's Moon Landing is an original game in that turn-by-turn spirit: you set a throttle, fire for five seconds, and read the gauges and the printed log. Nothing moves until you press Fire, so there is no time pressure. The numbers in our game are our own, chosen so that a careful landing is possible on every level.

In short: the first moon landing game was a patient one: think, type, and see what happens.
