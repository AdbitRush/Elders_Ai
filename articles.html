BrainPlay

Articles

Short articles on what research does and does not say about games, learning, attention, planning, music and daily routines, and a few history notes on the pastimes behind our games. Each one names its sources and states its limits, and some link to a game you can try.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

  • Make a game an invitationPlaying with someone as an occasion to talk, and what the small-group research can and cannot tell us.
  • A game is one part of your dayEnjoyment, a comfortable pace and the wider picture of healthy routines, and why a game score is not a diagnosis.
  • The pleasure of being a beginnerWhy learning something unfamiliar can be satisfying, what the large Synapse study did and did not show, and how to make a new rule approachable.
  • Why coming back can be different from starting overSpacing practice over time, what controlled studies of spaced retrieval found, and why a revisit should be a choice and not a chore.
  • Try to remember, then checkTrying to remember first, then seeing the answer, and why feedback matters more than the try alone.
  • Give a new name something to connect toA face-and-name mnemonic study, what its abstract can and cannot support, and keeping any memory aid kind and simple.
  • A quieter screen can be a better place to playDistraction research, why not all extra information is the same, and why a calmer screen is a design choice and not a treatment.
  • A plan is something you can changeA planning study with a null overall result, and how a puzzle can let you try a route, notice a problem and change it.
  • Play a rhythm, not a health promiseA review of musical instrument training with mixed results, and why a browser pattern game is play and not music therapy.
  • A small map to exploreNavigation-training research with its limits, and a small fictional map you can explore inside a puzzle.
  • What a crossword trial can tell usOne carefully studied crossword programme in people with mild cognitive impairment, and why that is a reason for accurate reporting and not a promise.
  • Let the player chooseA small game-design study about player control, and why choices in a game should be clear and reversible.

How to read them

The research articles are about related research. None of the studies they cite tested BrainPlay. Sources were checked on 2 and 3 November 2026; some studies were read as abstracts only, and each article says so where it matters. The history notes were checked on 4 November 2026 against the sources each one lists. The games linked from them are for entertainment and practice: they are not copies of the research programmes, not a medical test, and not a treatment.
