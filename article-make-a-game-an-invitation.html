Make a game an invitation

Evidence checked 2 November 2026. English only.

Please note: BrainPlay is for entertainment and practice. It has not been shown to prevent or treat dementia and is not a medical assessment.

A game can give people something to do together. You can explain a rule, compare different moves or laugh about an unexpected result. The conversation may be the part you value most.

That is a good reason to invite someone to play. It is different from claiming that a game treats loneliness or protects the brain.

What the research suggests

The National Institute on Aging encourages staying connected with family, friends and community. It describes social activity as part of healthy aging, while noting that evidence for lasting cognitive benefits from many engaging activities is not definitive. [1]

Some research has examined games specifically. In a pilot study involving 151 older adults at seven community activity centres in Israel, participants assigned to a group storytelling game reported improvements in loneliness and well-being after 12 weeks compared with a waiting-list group. [2]

The setting matters. Participants played in small groups and shared stories. This was not a study of someone playing a browser game alone. Two authors were affiliated with the game's studio, and our review checked the published abstract rather than the full paper. The study is a promising lead, not a reason to promise the same outcome from any game.

Another study followed a Scottish birth cohort and found that more frequent analog game playing was associated with less cognitive decline in later life. It accounted for childhood cognitive ability and several other factors. But it was observational: it cannot establish that playing games caused the difference, or that online games would have the same association. [3]

Try playing for the company

These are invitations, not a treatment plan:

  • Ask someone to sit with you for a game and talk through the choices.
  • Take turns deciding the next move rather than racing for a score.
  • Choose rules and a pace that everyone finds comfortable.
  • Stop or change games if someone is frustrated or would rather talk.

Playing alone can be enjoyable too. It simply should not be presented as a substitute for meaningful contact with other people.

The bottom line: A game can be an occasion to connect. BrainPlay itself has not been shown in these sources to reduce loneliness, improve well-being or prevent dementia.
